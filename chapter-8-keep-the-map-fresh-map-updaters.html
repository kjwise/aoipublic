Chapter 8 – Keep the Map Fresh (Map-Updaters)

Documentation, specifications, inventories, and policies drift because the systems they describe continue to change. A workflow can make selected drift visible and propose a bounded correction.

A Map-Updater is specifically a Terrain-to-Map workflow. It observes declared facts in the current system, compares them with one bounded Map surface, and proposes a reviewable Map change when they diverge. It does not change Terrain.

This direction matters. The same file can be authoritative for one property and observational for another. Route declarations can define one narrow property: the current endpoint inventory. They do not define intended API policy, compatibility promises, or future design. A Map-Updater must name the exact property for which the observed source is authoritative.

Sense, Normalize, Compare, Propose

The updater has four steps:

  1. Sense: observe the declared Terrain sources and fix their exact identities.
  2. Normalize: parse those observations into a stable representation, removing irrelevant transients.
  3. Compare: normalize the selected Map surface and produce an exact difference.
  4. Propose: when drift exists, create a candidate change inside the declared Map region.

All four steps may be deterministic. When structured Terrain can render the Map directly, that is preferable. If a model adds connective prose, the extracted facts remain fixed and the generated material stays inside a bounded candidate. The workflow then re-extracts and checks the factual structure before the proposal may close.

Alignment is a no-op only when every required source was available and the comparison completed. Missing or contradictory evidence cannot be interpreted as agreement.

Chapter 6’s Skeleton-First rule specializes cleanly here: deterministic extraction supplies the factual skeleton; probabilistic generation may supply only the permitted explanation or presentation. If generated structure is later mistaken for observed fact, the Map begins reinforcing its own invention. Protected re-extraction helps prevent that contamination by rejecting a mismatch before the proposal closes.

One Bounded Map Proposal

Consider a payments service whose local operator Map lists public routes, ownership, health checks, and standard commands. Route declarations, ownership metadata, deployment manifests, and task-runner definitions already contain those facts.

Contract element Declaration
Observed sources that define this generated property Fixed route declarations, ownership metadata, deployment manifests, and command definitions
Permitted effect Edit only the generated inventory blocks in the operator Map
Required evidence Source identities, normalized comparison, bounded candidate identity, and complete check results
Required checks Every source parses; no out-of-region edit occurs; the candidate’s extracted inventory exactly matches Terrain
Adoption A separate review or policy decision decides whether the successful proposal becomes the Map

A representative candidate remains small:

 ## Health Checks
- Readiness: `/ready`
+ Readiness: `/readyz`
 ## Ownership
- Primary: `payments-platform`
+ Primary: `money-movement`

The updater can establish that drift existed, that the patch stayed inside the permitted Map blocks, and that its extracted inventory matches the declared Terrain sources. It cannot establish that the organization chose the right owners, routes, or operating policy. Those are different properties with different authority.

The run may close as a successful proposal after its required checks pass. The candidate is not adopted merely because it was generated, retained, or sealed.

The Opposite Direction

A Map-Updater always runs from observed Terrain toward a proposed Map revision. The inverse is Map-driven reconciliation: an authoritative Map governs a bounded proposal to change Terrain.

Direction Fixed authority Proposed effect
Terrain → Map Declared observed facts for one Map property A bounded Map revision
Map → Terrain An adopted requirement or desired state A bounded Terrain revision

Both directions may cross between natural-language and formal representations, but they are not inverses. Map-driven reconciliation elaborates adopted intent into a Terrain candidate. A Map-Updater describes selected facts observed in Terrain. It cannot recover why the system was built, infer what policy ought to be, or make its description authoritative.

If policy requires encrypted storage and a Sensor observes an unencrypted bucket, Map-driven reconciliation may propose the infrastructure change. The policy remains fixed during that run.

If evidence suggests the policy itself is wrong, the reconciliation workflow must not edit its own authority to make Terrain pass. It stops for an external decision. A separately activated Map-Updater may then propose a policy revision under the governance rules for that Map surface.

The two directions may exchange evidence, but neither reverses direction in flight. This prevents a candidate from changing the rule that judges it.

Updater Success Is Not Build Policy

The updater and the delivery pipeline answer different questions. The Map-Updater may succeed because it produced a checked proposal. The pipeline may still block because the currently adopted Map remains out of sync until that proposal is reviewed and adopted.

That separation avoids a false failure. The updater completed its declared work; the enclosing policy decides whether unresolved drift may proceed. A waiver, where permitted, is a separate governed decision with exact scope, rationale, approval, and expiry.

For factual Map regions, validation should re-extract the candidate’s structure and bind it to the declared sources. Structural lint and reference checks may support that comparison. Subjective prose receives a proportionate review gate. No tool list can prove every sentence true; the invariant is that each enforced property has a declared basis and an independent check.

Maps as Operating Memory

At small scales, a Map may be a schema, interface inventory, or policy file. At larger scales, Maps include architecture decisions, operating rules, incident lessons, onboarding guidance, and other shared knowledge.

Not every surface should become a hard gate. Structured contracts often support exact comparison. Judgment-heavy material may produce an advisory proposal that still requires human review. Different Map surfaces need checks proportionate to the claims they make.

The context-quality properties from Chapter 6 still apply: a useful Map concentrates decision-bearing meaning, remains traceable to current evidence, and stays relevant to the governed property. A Map is fresh only for the surfaces that were actually compared. More text does not make it current, and synchronization does not make every claim true.

Historical evidence can also support Map proposals. Repeated incidents may justify a runbook change; recurring review findings may justify a new check. Evidence does not install those lessons by itself. The appropriate Map-Updater produces a candidate, and independent adoption makes it authoritative.

A synchronized Map can then support context selection, generated help, policy evaluation, or later workflows. Chapter 13 examines the stronger boundary where a selected Map claim becomes an enforceable constraint. Synchronization prepares that boundary, but does not create it automatically.
